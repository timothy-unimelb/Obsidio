The Obsidio Line

How a small backend went from buckling under load to serving every order on time — told as the kitchen it really is, one fix at a time.

First — the kitchen

One tiny kitchen, three dishes

You're running a small price-analytics API, and it lives in a two-chef kitchen: 2 CPU cores and 2 GB of memory, total — you can't hire a third chef or rent a bigger room. All through service, orders pour in from a fixed crowd in a set 6 : 3 : 1 mix, cheap to expensive:

The drink
GET /price

Pre-poured: look up a symbol's latest price and hand it back. An in-memory lookup — almost no work.

60% of orders·worth 1
The salad
GET /stats

Chopped to order: mean, min, max and standard deviation over 500 recent prices — recomputed every time.

30% of orders·worth 3
The braise
GET /risk

A 50,000-step slow cook: hash a seed with SHA-256, feed the result back in, repeat 50,000 times. Deliberately expensive.

10% of orders·worth 10

Here's the trap: the braise is so slow it can clog the whole line — so even a drink that needs no work ends up stuck behind it. Your score is the weighted count of orders served correctly and on time. So the goal is easy to say and hard to do: keep the fast dishes flying while the braise simmers — on two chefs. Every card below is one move toward that.

the nine moves

01The brigade

Too many cooks spoil the broth

Under its cap the container can still see every core on the host, so the runtime spins up workers for cores it can't use — and they stall on the ones that aren't really there. Pin the count to the two you actually get.

ResultGOMAXPROCS=2 — foundational. Stops the runtime overscheduling onto phantom cores.

02The pass

A counter that skips the line

A price lookup does almost no work, yet in one shared queue it sits behind a 50,000-round braise. Give /price and /stats their own path that never enters the risk queue.

ResultStructural win. The fast path stops waiting on the slow one — its p95 collapses.

03Technique

Braise without the mess

The naive braise built a fresh string and byte slice on every one of its 50,000 rounds — a mountain of garbage that kept the collector busy. Reuse a single fixed buffer instead.

Result~150,000 → 0 allocations per request. Keeps the garbage collector off the hot path.

04Batching

One stir, many pots

After each hash the CPU waits on the result before starting the next. Run several independent hashes at once and one chain's work fills another's wait.

Result+9% arm64 / +2% x86 (per-chain −25% / −5%). Bigger where the CPU has more latency to hide.

05The equipment

The pro range — used only where the kitchen has one

Most server CPUs have a built-in SHA-256 instruction — a commercial range that cooks two pots at once. But not every grading machine will. So detect it at startup, take the fast branch where it exists, and fall back to plain code where it doesn't. You never assume the equipment is there.

Result+22% — separated x86. Per-chain cost −52%. The first big structural win, with zero portability risk.

06The twist

A faster part, a flat whole

Moving the hex formatting into the kernel removed a per-round hand-off and cut hash cost 10%… yet the score didn't move. The braise was no longer the bottleneck — the fast path was. Optimizing a part that isn't the constraint changes nothing.

The lessonMeasure the whole system, not the part you changed. This flat result is what pointed straight to the next fix.

07The payoff

Watch the window

The hashing loop couldn't be interrupted, so quick requests waited behind a whole braise. Pause it briefly between chunks to hand out whatever is waiting, then resume — the braise loses nothing.

Result+54% — separated x86. Drink p95 43 ms → 10 ms. The single biggest win.

08The door

A quick no beats a long wait

In a rush you can't clear, the oldest order will blow its deadline even if you serve it now — and a customer parked at the stove orders nothing. So serve the newest who can still make it, and turn the overflow away at the door in a millisecond, spending a small error budget to do it. A customer waved off comes straight back and orders drinks; one left waiting earns you nothing. The budget is charged in the same breath as the decision, so a crowd of waiters can't all slip through at once. And the rare order that ages past its patience while the budget is spent is simply held: we tried serving those late, and at four times the published load it pushed the slow-dish p95 past its bar — so it was measured, and reverted.

Result+17% — separated x86. 4.14M → 4.85M at 0.85% errors (gate: 1%); risk p95 215 → 80 ms. At 4× the published peak it still passes every bar at 0.88% errors and a risk p95 of 167 ms. Same design as Advait's governor; head-to-head on one box, ours serves +12% at the same error budget because the drink counter answers in 3.4 ms rather than 7.

09The bonus

A blackout-proof book

The bonus rewards surviving a hard restart. No database needed: append each price update to a file and flush it to disk before replying — the chef writes it in the book before saying "done" — then replay the file on startup. The scored siege is read-only, so it never pays this cost.

ResultSurvives a hard kill. Free on the scored path — pure additive bonus.
